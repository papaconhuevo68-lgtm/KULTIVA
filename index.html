the problems in Ayacucho

The problem in numbers

Decisions are made without information about actual weather or demand. The result: oversupply, late pests, and contracts being cancelled.

Overproduction · Chiara

S/ 0.50 vs S/ 0.90

Everyone plants the same crop and prices fall to S/ 0.50/kg (a loss-making sale). Farmers who planted early were able to sell up to S/ 0.90/kg on-farm.

Adjusting planting dates and coordinating output protects income
Weather and ENSO · 2026

October vs January

Heat accelerated the maturation of prickly pear in Ayacucho from January to late October 2026. Traditional dates no longer provide a reliable warning.

Relationship with ENFEN / SENAMHI still to be confirmed
Plant health and export

EU Reg. 2018/848

Humidity and heat trigger late blight, moths, and mildew. Rotating with legumes reduces pests and is required to sell organic quinoa in Europe.

Ayacucho: 1st organic quinoa producer in Peru

From orbit to fair contracts

The solution in a single chain

Six connected steps where satellite data becomes healthy soil and secure income.

01 · Virtual sensor

The satellite is the sensor

NASA data measures rainfall, temperature, soil moisture, and plant vigor without installing hardware in the field.

02 · Diagnosis

Data becomes risk

We detect frost, drought, accumulated heat (harvest), and conditions favorable for blight, moths, or mildew.

03 · Communication

Risk becomes an alert

Farmers receive the warning days in advance via WhatsApp or voice note in Spanish and Ayacucho Quechua.

04 · Action

The alert leads to a decision

Clear, jargon-free recommendation: harvest now or wait, and which crop to rotate in the next season.

05 · Regeneration

The decision improves the soil

Rotating with legumes (tarwi or broad bean) restores natural nitrogen, reduces pests, and supports organic certification.

06 · Regional scale

Guides the season

Cooperatives and the Regional Agriculture Directorate coordinate planting to meet volumes and organic certification requirements.

adaptation path

Users and adaptation path

We start with those who already have technicians, scale, and need. The farmer receives the recommendation without needing to learn how to use a satellite map.

Level 1 · Main channel

Cooperatives and the Regional Government (DRA)

They plan the season, record rotation, and assess oversupply risk to meet volume and organic certification targets.

Level 2 · Training

Technicians and community leaders

They use visual views in workshops (like the Vidas project) to explain why rotation is needed using data from their own area.

Level 3 · End user

The farmer

Zero setup; receives a clear card in Spanish and Quechua explaining what to plant, when, and why.

Future vision (outside the demo scope): buyers, investors, and rural banks.

Farmer-centered

Key criterion of the Field Shift challenge

Real constraints

Rainfed or irrigated land, family labor, and self-consumption are direct inputs for the planner.

Simple language

The card explains the cause in Spanish and Quechua without technical jargon.

Human decision

The satellite is only a support tool; final validation is done by the farmer and technician.

Validity and scientific honesty

Open NASA data and clearly stated limits

NASA POWER (~55 km) NASADEM (30 m) SMAP & GPM IMERG HLS / Sentinel-2 (10–30 m) FAO GAEZ & PISCO SENAMHI

What we do

  • • We estimate climate risk and conditions favorable for pests.
  • • We declare the scale: regional climate (~55 km) vs field-scale (30 m).
  • • We label each datum as Observed, Estimated, or No evidence.

What we do not promise

  • • We do not predict the future exactly: we issue risk alerts.
  • • We do not “see” the pest from space; field validation is required.
  • • We do we don't set the market prices.

Evidence rule: if there is no parameter validated by INIA or a local technician for Ayacucho, Kultura states “insufficient evidence” and never invents data.
